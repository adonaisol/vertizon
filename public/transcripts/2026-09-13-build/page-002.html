Assistant2026-09-13T19:07:25.033Z

Those three problems have one root cause, which gives us the non-obvious angle.

The insight: comparing ratings between managers doesn't reveal who is lenient. One manager's team may simply be stronger. You only see leniency when you compare rating against evidence, meaning the written examples scored against one shared rubric. With that shift, your three problems each show up as a distinct pattern: - Rating doesn't match evidence: individual employees sitting far from the norm. - Lenient vs harsh managers: one manager's ratings running consistently higher or lower than other managers' for the same strength of evidence. - Standards drift: managers setting the bar for "Exceeds" at different amounts of evidence.

This is also how ML teams audit human labelers, which ties straight to the role's "evals" work.

The catch is that the model mapping evidence to the rubric is itself a rater. It needs checking: whether it gives stable results, whether it cites real quotes, and whether it's fooled by managers who simply write more. Taking that problem seriously is what makes this a Staff-level submission rather than a dashboard.

Three approaches:

A. Evidence-vs-rating calibration map (recommended) - Who it's for: the HR partner or facilitator preparing for a calibration meeting. - Data: a bundled synthetic company with about 25 employees, 4 managers, a leveling rubric, and a rating plus written review for each person. - What Claude does: pulls out each piece of evidence, tags it to a rubric dimension and scope level, and quotes the exact passage. - The main view: one scatter plot with evidence strength on one axis and rating on the other, with a trend line per manager. All three problems are visible at once, and clicking any point shows the quotes behind it. - Human control: users can override any piece of evidence and the chart updates. - Output: a ranked list of the cases most worth the meeting's limited time. - Trade-off: the evidence-scoring step has to be trustworthy. That's the point of the design, but it's also the main risk.

B. "Under another manager's bar" comparison - Pick one employee and see how their evidence would likely be rated under each manager's implied standard. - Trade-off: it's the most striking idea, but it's narrower. It also depends on inferring each manager's standard from few examples, which is statistically shaky with only 5–8 reports each.

C. Shared test cases, borrowed from labeling operations - Before calibration, every manager rates the same 5 synthetic reviews. Their differences on those shared cases measure leniency and drift directly, and Claude explains the disagreements using the rubric. - Trade-off: no real employee is ever assessed by AI, and it's the cleanest way to measure. But it's a process change more than a tool, the demo is basically a survey, and it makes the least use of the model.

I recommend A, with C as a strong "with more time" addition. A tackles all three of your problems in one main view and puts the model-as-rater problem front and centre.

Which approach do you want, or would you mix them?

Yes, it's workable, and cheaper than it sounds: the drilldown falls out of the map almost for free.

Why it's cheap. The map already fits one line per manager (rating against evidence strength). "Under Manager X's bar" is just reading this employee's evidence score off Manager X's line. No new model call, no new data; one small panel that reuses the fit. That also makes the two views agree by construction, which matters when someone challenges a number in the meeting.

Two things to handle so it stays credible:

  1. Small samples. With 5–8 reports per manager, the implied bar is uncertain. Show it as a range, not a point ("likely Meets–Exceeds under Priya"), and say what it's based on ("from Priya's 7 other ratings"). Hiding that would be the kind of false precision a Staff reviewer would catch immediately.
  2. Framing. "How other managers would rate you" can read as AI ranking people, or as shaming a lenient manager. Frame it as a question about the standard, not the person: "Is this rating a property of the evidence, or of who wrote it?" The drilldown answers that for one case, which is exactly what a calibration facilitator needs when a name comes up.

With those, the combination is stronger than A alone: the map finds the cases worth discussing, the drilldown gives the facilitator a defensible way to open the discussion.
